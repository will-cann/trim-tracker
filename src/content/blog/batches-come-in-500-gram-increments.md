If you ask an extraction lead how large a batch is, the answer is almost always a round number: 500 grams, 1,000 grams, or 1,500 grams. It is never "whatever came off the wash." There is a practical reason for this, and it is worth understanding because it shapes most of the planning in a solventless program.

The reason is the cost of lab testing. In most states a compliance test runs around $500 per batch regardless of how large the batch is, and the minimum batch size the lab will accept is typically 500 grams. If you send a 300-gram batch, you pay the full fee to test 60 percent as much product. Because of this, programs do not run batches under 500 grams, and most prefer to work at around 1,000.

The wash process fits this naturally. A single ice-water wash of fresh frozen material generally yields somewhere near 500 grams of bubble hash. Since no one stops between washes of the same strain, two or three washes are usually combined into one batch of 1,000 to 1,500 grams and sent for a single test.

Equipment sets the upper limit. Every wash vessel has an input capacity. One common commercial unit holds about 17,000 grams of fresh frozen, and at a 3 to 4 percent yield that works out to roughly one batch of hash per fill. So batch planning starts from what the machine holds rather than from an estimate of demand.

From these facts, three working rules follow:

- **Do not test under 500 grams.** If a wash comes up short, combine it with the next one until you clear the minimum.
- **Plan in 500-gram steps.** When you work backward from demand (how many grams of rosin a customer needs), round up to the next 500-gram increment and then convert that back into pounds of biomass to wash.
- **Keep the batch size the same from run to run.** Yield comparisons between strains or between suppliers are only meaningful when the batches are the same size. A 4 percent yield on a 1,500-gram batch and a 4 percent yield on a 400-gram batch do not tell you the same thing.

To give a sense of what consistency looks like in practice: three recent batches from one program, each from a full fill of about 17 kilograms of the same strain, finished at 474, 502, and 497 grams. That is the kind of spread you should expect from a standardized batch. When a number falls well outside it, you can be reasonably confident the cause was the material rather than the method.
