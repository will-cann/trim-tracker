When we ran a trim room, the records were kept on paper. Each batch had a sheet, and on the sheet went the starting weight, the names of the people working the batch, and the amounts that came off the table as flower, small bud, trim, and waste. At the end of the day the sheets went to our compliance administrator. He typed them into a spreadsheet, checked that the weights added up, and then entered the results into METRC.

This meant we were keeping four separate inventories: the paper sheets, the spreadsheet, our digital inventory system, and METRC. Keeping them in agreement was a job in itself, and at larger facilities we have seen it become more than one job. The people doing it were not growing or trimming. They were comparing paper against a database and chasing down the differences. That is the first cost of paper, and it is significant, but it is also the easy one to see.

The cost that took us longer to recognize was accountability.

Trim crews are often brought in from a third party, and the people on the floor change from week to week. Product can leave the room in amounts too small to notice on any given day. The phrase we used for it on the floor was "a nug in the glove, a glove in the pocket": a nug goes into a glove, the glove goes into a pocket, and it leaves at lunch. No one takes a pound. Over a season, though, small amounts across many people add up to a real number, and the phrase is a reminder that this kind of loss is invisible one day at a time.

Paper has no way to catch this. A sheet records what someone wrote down, often hours after the fact, in handwriting the administrator then has to interpret. It cannot tell you at two in the afternoon that what has come off the table so far does not add up to what went onto it. By the time the spreadsheet shows a gap, the batch has been packaged, the crew has rotated, and there is no way to work out where the difference went.

The change that fixes this is to weigh what goes onto the table and what comes off it, per person and per bucket, at the time it happens. When the record is kept that way, there is nothing left to reconcile at the end of the day, because the sheet has been checking itself all along.

That has three effects. The compliance administrator receives a finished number rather than a stack of sheets. Cultivation receives an actual yield rather than an estimate. And the crew knows the count is live, which does most of the work of accountability on its own.

We want to be clear that we did not make this change because we distrusted trimmers. We made it because a room with four inventories and no live count was unfair to the honest people in it. When a gap turned up, it landed on everyone, because there was no way to show who had been doing their job correctly.

How we handle it: that trim room is the reason the first thing we built was a trim tracker.
