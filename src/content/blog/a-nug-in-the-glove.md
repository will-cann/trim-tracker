The trim room ran on paper. Every batch had a sheet: start weight, who was on it, what came off as flower, small bud, trim, waste. At the end of the day the sheets went to our compliance administrator, who typed them into a spreadsheet, checked the weights, and entered the results into METRC.

So we had four inventories. Paper. The spreadsheet. The digital inventory system. METRC. There were people whose entire job was reconciling them — not growing, not trimming, reconciling pieces of paper against a database. That is the obvious cost, and it is a big one.

The less obvious cost was accountability.

Trim crews are often third party. The faces change week to week. And product leaves the room in amounts too small to notice on any given day: a nug in the glove, the glove in the pocket, gone at lunch. Nobody takes a pound. Everyone, over a season, adds up.

Paper cannot catch that. A sheet records what someone wrote down, hours after the fact, in handwriting the admin has to interpret. It cannot tell you at two in the afternoon that what has come off the table so far does not add up to what went onto it. By the time the spreadsheet shows a gap, the batch is packaged, the crew has rotated, and the gap is a mystery nobody can solve.

> If you weigh what goes onto the table and what comes off it — per person, per bucket, when it happens — the sheet stops needing to be reconciled. It reconciles itself as the day goes.

That is the whole change. The compliance admin gets a number instead of a stack. Cultivation gets a yield instead of a guess. And the crew knows the scale is running, which is most of the accountability on its own. People behave differently when the count is live.

We did not fix this because we distrusted trimmers. We fixed it because a room with four inventories and no live count was unfair to the honest ones, too — any gap landed on everyone.

How we handle it: that trim room is the reason the first thing we ever built was a trim tracker.
