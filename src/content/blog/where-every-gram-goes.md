Ask a trim lead what their yield was last week and you will get a number. Ask why it was three points lower than the week before and the answer gets vague. Different strain. New hire. Machine was down. Probably.

The gap between the number and the explanation is an accounting problem, not a cultivation one. Most trim floors track what went in and what came out. Very few track who did what to it in between.

## Four buckets

Every gram that enters a trim session leaves it as one of four things:

| Bucket | What it is |
|--------|-----------|
| **Flower** | Finished, saleable bud |
| **Shake** | Small bud and loose material, still product |
| **Trim** | Sugar leaf and fan trim, extraction input |
| **Waste** | Stem and anything unusable |

Four buckets is not a novel idea. What changes the picture is tracking those four buckets **per trimmer**, inside a session that can hold more than one batch.

## Per-person, per-batch

A NeuroCann trim session is one live surface with multiple batch entries on it. Each entry is a harvest — strain, license, start weight. Each entry has trimmers assigned to it, with a start time, an end time, and whether they are working scissors or machine.

Weights go in per trimmer: flower, shake, trim, waste. The session rolls those up into a stacked progress bar against the start weight, so at any moment you can see how much of the batch has landed in each bucket and how much is still on the table.

That one change answers the "why" question. Yield dipped because the machine lane ran a strain with dense, small bud and shake ran high. Or because two new trimmers were on the same batch and their waste ratio was double the floor average. The data says which.

## The shift does not end when the batch does

Trim does not fit neatly into eight hours. A batch gets started at two in the afternoon and is half done when the crew leaves. The next morning somebody rebuilds the spreadsheet, re-enters the start weight, and tries to remember who had already logged what.

NeuroCann handles this with a rollover rule. When a session is submitted, any batch that is still upcoming or active carries into a new session automatically. Trimmer assignments and logged weights come with it. The morning crew opens the app and the batch is where they left it.

## From bin to package

Trim sits in the middle of the facility, and the session reflects that on both ends.

**Upstream**, ready harvest bins pull directly into a session. A bin that has been curing, burped, and marked ready becomes a trim entry without re-keying the strain, license, or weight.

**Downstream**, trimmed material becomes packages. Flower out of a submitted batch can be packaged from the session itself, carrying the strain and harvest reference forward into inventory.

One product, one chain of custody from hanging to tagged package.

## By voice, same as everywhere

The trim floor talks to the system the same way the harvest floor does. "Add Maria to the Gelato batch with scissors" proposes a trimmer assignment for review. "Maria's flower is 340 grams" proposes a weight. Confirm, and the bar moves.

## What this is not

It is not a timeclock or a piece-rate payroll system. Start and end times are there for productivity, not for paying people. It is not a hardware scale integration — numbers are entered or spoken, then confirmed. And it does not auto-submit packages to METRC; it gets the record right so the submission is clean when you make it.

## The report at the end

Because weights are tracked per person and per batch, the reports module can answer questions a yield number cannot. Grams per hour by trimmer. Flower-to-shake ratio by strain. Labor cost per pound. Not estimates — the actual accounting from the floor.

That is what "where every gram goes" means in practice. Not a slogan about precision. A session that knows, for every batch and every person, which of four buckets each gram landed in.
